Which Embedding Should a World Model Plan In? Research Proposal

Michael Ramirez, early draft of 2026-10-27. The related papers have been checked at abstract or results-table level only; this proposal is not final.

In one line: latent world models such as DINO-WM plan inside a frozen encoder's embedding space. The choice of encoder can change planning success several-fold, but it is made by habit. We want a cheap test that, before any world model is trained, says which embedding will plan well.

Our bet (speculative): a good planning space is one where the robot's actions explain most of how the embedding changes from frame to frame. Probes of what is in a single frame don't measure that.

Where we are: idea stage, nothing run. We can reuse the probe code and cached LIBERO features from the stacking-encoders project, and published papers give per-encoder planning results to test against.

Labels: verified = we ran it or checked it in the source; recalled = from memory, not checked; speculative = our hypothesis.

Motivation

The encoder a latent world model plans in decides a large part of its success, yet nobody can tell which encoder will work without training a world model on each one.

How these models work. A frozen encoder (e.g. DINOv2) turns each camera frame into an embedding. A small predictor learns "embedding now + action gives embedding next". To plan, the model tries many action sequences in imagination and keeps the one whose predicted embedding ends closest to the goal image's embedding (DINO-WM, ICML 2025).

The encoder matters a lot:

  • DINO-WM, PushT success: DINOv2 patch features 0.90, DINOv2 CLS token 0.44, R3M 0.42, ImageNet ResNet 0.20. On Wall: 0.96 vs 0.58, 0.34 and 0.12 (verified, DINO-WM Table 2).
  • Planning Limits (2026), how many steps ahead a goal can be and still be planned reliably: DINOv2 and V-JEPA 2.1 reach 10, while V-JEPA 2, VideoMAEv2 and VideoPrism reach 5 (verified, Table 2, threshold 10).
  • ALPS (2026), same DINO-WM protocol: Reacher success ranges from 74.0 (SigLIP 2) to 86.0 (DINOv3); on Rope, C-RADIOv3 is best and SigLIP 2 worst (verified, Table 3).

The rankings don't follow the usual signs of a good encoder:

  • No encoder wins every task. In ALPS, a different encoder leads on Rope (C-RADIOv3), Reacher (DINOv3) and Granular (ALPS) (verified, Table 3).
  • ALPS reports that "tokens that yield the lowest single-frame readout error are not always the most predictable under learned dynamics" (verified through a page-summarising fetch; quote not hand-checked). The standard probe of what is in one frame does not tell you how well an embedding supports dynamics.
  • DINO-WM-style planners break when the background or lighting changes, even though the physics hasn't changed (Toso et al. 2026, abstract). The embedding spends capacity on things the robot cannot control.

Today, choosing an encoder means training and evaluating a world model per encoder per task (speculative: none of the papers we read states that cost).

Problem statement

We want a cheap, training-free score, computed from frozen embeddings of recorded robot trajectories, that ranks encoders by how well a latent world model will plan in them.

Research question (draft): does the share of embedding change that is caused by the action predict planning success across encoders better than existing single-frame probes?

  • Controllable change: the part of the frame-to-frame embedding change that knowing the action lets you predict. Example: the arm and the pushed block move.
  • Uncontrollable change: the rest. Examples: lighting flicker, camera noise, a distractor moving.
  • Action recoverability: how well the action can be read back from two consecutive embeddings (an inverse-dynamics probe).

Intuition: the planner compares imagined futures. If most of the embedding's movement isn't caused by actions, different action sequences look alike and the planner can't tell good from bad (speculative).

Related works

Recent papers show that action-relevance matters by training it into world models, and others benchmark encoders by training a world model on each. None we found measures action-relevance on frozen embeddings to choose an encoder before training.

Method

We score each frozen encoder from recorded trajectories with a few simple linear models, then check whether the scores predict how well a world model plans in that encoder.

  1. Collect trajectories with actions. Use public data: DINO-WM's PushT, Wall and PointMaze datasets (downloadable per its GitHub README), and LIBERO, which we already have.
  2. Embed every frame with each frozen encoder, 8 to 15 encoders: DINOv2, DINOv3, SigLIP 2, C-RADIO, SAM 3, VGGT-Ω, V-JEPA 2 and 2.1, and others. Five loaders already exist in our stacking-encoders code.
  3. Compute cheap scores per encoder, all with ridge regression on held-out trajectories:
        Controllable share: how much better can we predict the next embedding change if we also know the action? (prediction with the action minus prediction without)
        Action recoverability: how well can the action be read back from two consecutive embeddings?
        Multi-step versions of both, 1, 5 and 10 steps ahead, because planners look several steps ahead.
  4. Make the comparison fair. Encoders have different sizes (768 to 2,304 dimensions), so we reduce all to the same dimension and tune regularization on validation data. Confidence intervals resample whole trajectories, not frames.
  5. Check against planning success. Rank encoders by each score and compare with their real planning success: first the published numbers (DINO-WM, ALPS, Planning Limits), then our own DINO-WM runs with 3 seeds per encoder.

Baselines the score must beat: a single-frame state probe (what ALPS uses), similarity to DINOv2 (CKA), a single-frame action probe, and a random ranking. As an expensive reference, we also compare with the trained world model's own prediction error.

Expected results

We expect the controllable-share score to rank encoders by planning success better than single-frame probes, and to drop when distractors are added. This is speculative until E1 runs.

What we hope to see:

  • The score reproduces known gaps without training, such as DINOv2 patch features above the CLS token on PushT.
  • It beats the single-frame state probe at predicting planning success.
  • It falls under background and lighting changes for encoders whose planning also falls.

What would count against us: if the single-frame probe ranks encoders as well as our score, action-controllability adds nothing measurable, and we report that. If no cheap score beats random, the differences between encoders come from something linear models on frozen features can't see.

Open questions

  • ALPS, Planning Limits and AD-WM need a full read. AD-WM's "elite regret" diagnostic may already capture part of this idea.
  • Training cost of DINO-WM per encoder on Nautilus (not yet measured).
  • The DINO-WM datasets have no stated license on its GitHub page; this needs checking before use.
  • Is this best as its own paper, or as a second study that shares tools with the stacking-encoders project?
