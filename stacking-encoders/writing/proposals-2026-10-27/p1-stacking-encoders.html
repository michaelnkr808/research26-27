Stacking Encoders in World Action Models: Research Proposal

Michael Ramirez, draft of 2026-10-27

In one line: adding a second frozen vision encoder to a World Action Model (WAM) sometimes helps and sometimes costs 5 or more success points, and nobody can tell which before training. We want to explain why and predict it cheaply.

Where we are: the simplest diagnostic (similarity and input-side probes on frozen features) has been tested and does not flag the harmful combination (4 pairs). A second, target-side diagnostic is coded and passes synthetic tests but has not been run on real features. Everything under Expected results is an expectation, not a finding.

Labels: verified = we ran it or checked it in the source paper; recalled = from memory, not yet checked; speculative = our hypothesis or guess.

Motivation

Stacking frozen encoders into a WAM is unpredictable, and the only way to find out is to train the whole model.

  • Encoders help a lot on their own. In Liu et al. (2026), adding frozen DINOv3 features to a Wan 2.2-based WAM raises LIBERO-Plus success from 74.7 to 84.0 (verified, Table 1).
  • Adding a second one is a gamble. Of the paper's 16 "add one more encoder" steps, 11 gain 0.2 to 2.1 points, 1 is flat (−0.03) and 4 lose 0.4 to 5.4 points (verified, our arithmetic on Table 10). The worst: DINOv3 + C-RADIOv4 drops to 78.6, below both encoders alone (84.0 and 83.5).
  • The effect depends on context. VGGT-Ω adds +0.4 on top of DINOv3 but costs −2.4 on top of DINOv3 + UniTok + V-JEPA 2.1 (verified, Table 10). So the useful question is not "is encoder B good?" but "is B good given what is already there?"
  • The explanation today is a story, not a measurement. The paper attributes the C-RADIOv4 drop to a teacher-student overlap (RADIO distills DINOv3, SigLIP 2 and SAM 3) and gives no metric (verified, Sec. 4.4). Each number comes from one trained checkpoint, so training noise is unknown (verified, App. C.2 and E.3).
  • The obvious cheap check fails. On our own frozen features (LIBERO libero_goal, 4 DINOv3 pairs), neither similarity (CKA) nor "how much B adds to an action probe" flags C-RADIOv4. C-RADIOv4 is the most similar to DINOv3 and also adds the most to the probe (verified, our tally).

Each combination needs a full WAM training run, and the paper released no code or checkpoints (verified). A cheap test that says "don't stack these two" before training would save most of that compute (speculative).

Problem statement

We want a cheap test, run on frozen features before any WAM training, that predicts whether adding encoder B to encoder A will help or hurt, and that explains the harmful cases.

Research question (draft): when frozen encoder features are stacked as both the inputs and the prediction targets of a WAM, why do some combinations hurt, and can a diagnostic on frozen features predict which ones will?

Two terms we keep separate:

  • Similarity: how alike two encoders' features are on the same frames (symmetric; measured with CKA).
  • Complementarity: useful information B carries that A doesn't, for a named task. It is asymmetric: what B adds to A is not what A adds to B.

Low similarity is not the same as complementarity: two encoders can differ in ways the robot doesn't need.

Related works

Other work either fuses encoders and judges the result by training, or scores combinations without training on single-frame classification tasks. None predicts the effect of fusing frozen features into a model that also has to predict those features.

Method

In Liu et al.'s WAM, an encoder's features are used twice. They are fed in as a clue about the current frame, and the model must also predict them for future frames (verified, Sec. 3.3, App. B.2). Most of DINOv3's benefit comes from the prediction role: 76.5 with input only, 81.9 with prediction only, 84.0 with both (verified, Table 9). Our first check only tested the input role, which is why it couldn't see the harm.

Main hypothesis (H-E, speculative): a partner encoder hurts when it makes the prediction target worse. Either its future features are hard to predict, or its loss is large enough to crowd out the model's ability to predict DINOv3's future.

  1. Extract frozen features once from robot demos, for a base encoder (DINOv3) and its partners. No model training. Done for 5 encoders on one LIBERO suite.
  2. Measure each pair cheaply, three ways, with simple linear models on those features:
    • Loss share: how big is B's piece of the combined target? (variance only, no fitting)
    • Predictability: how well can B's future features be predicted from the current frame?
    • Interference: give a small, capacity-limited model the job of predicting D's future alone, then D's and B's future together. How much worse does it get at D's part? We run this with raw feature scales and with each encoder rescaled to equal size. If rescaling removes the harm, the fix is a normalization step.
  3. Rank the pairs by each measure and compare with measured gains: first the paper's table, then our own small policies trained with 3 or more seeds per combination, because the paper's numbers come from one checkpoint each.
  4. Test the cause directly: if interference points at a pair, train that pair with and without per-encoder normalization.

All measures are compared against baselines: CKA, B's score on its own, the paper's "similar training objectives" reasoning, KAGES-style marginal alignment, and a random ranking.

Expected results

We expect a target-side measure to flag harmful pairs that similarity and input-side probes miss, and per-encoder normalization to remove some of the harm. All of this is speculative until E2 runs.

What we evaluateMetricWhy this metric
Does a diagnostic order combos like the real gains?Spearman rank correlation and Kendall τ (as in Agostinelli et al.)Only the order matters for choosing combos; the scales differ (probe R² vs success %)
Does it catch harmful combos?Share of harmful steps flagged as harmfulAvoiding a bad stack is the most useful decision

Policy ground truth (E4, E5)LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus success rate, mean ± std over 3 or more training seedsStandard benchmark metric; seeds fix the one-checkpoint problem

What we hope to see:

  • E2: C-RADIOv4 shows the most interference with DINOv3's future prediction under raw scaling, and less after equalizing scales.
  • E3 and E4: the interference measure ranks combos better than CKA, single-encoder score and random, including the 3 harmful 3- and 4-encoder steps.
  • E5: normalizing per encoder recovers part of the DINOv3 + C-RADIOv4 drop.

What would count against us, and what we'd report instead:

  • C-RADIOv4 is not the top interferer: H-E fails on frozen features. That is still reportable: neither input-side nor target-side frozen-feature tests predict stacking harm.
  • Our multi-seed policies don't reproduce the DINOv3 + C-RADIOv4 drop: the paper's harm may be single-checkpoint noise, which is also worth reporting.
  • With 7 pairs (16 steps), we report tallies and rank correlations with CIs, not significance claims.

Open questions

  • Is the target-side framing (H-E) the right main hypothesis, or should the input-side negative result be the paper?
  • Is a small multi-seed policy on cached features an acceptable stand-in for full WAM training, if it reproduces the paper's ordering?
  • Venue and timeline: workshop paper first, or a main conference?
  • Should we ask Liu et al. for training-seed variance on DINOv3 + C-RADIOv4 and for their (unstated) feature normalization?
