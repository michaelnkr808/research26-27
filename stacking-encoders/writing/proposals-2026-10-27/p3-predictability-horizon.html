How Far Ahead Can an Embedding Be Predicted? Research Proposal

Michael Ramirez, short early draft of 2026-10-27. Related papers checked at abstract or results-table level only.

In one line: latent world models only plan well a few steps ahead, and that range depends on the encoder. We want to measure, without training a world model, how quickly each frozen encoder's features become unpredictable, and test whether that predicts the planning range.

Labels: verified = checked in the source; speculative = our hypothesis.

Motivation

  • Planning Limits (2026) finds that latent world models guide action choice reliably only for goals about 5 to 10 steps ahead, while task goals are 16 to 53 steps away (verified, abstract).
  • The range depends on the encoder: at their threshold of 10, DINOv2 and V-JEPA 2.1 reach 10 steps, while V-JEPA 2, VideoMAEv2 and VideoPrism reach 5 (verified, Table 2).
  • A larger predictor (81 times larger) and longer training rollouts do not extend the range (verified, abstract). So part of the limit may sit in the embedding itself, not in the model trained on it (speculative).

Problem statement

Measure a "predictability horizon" for each frozen encoder: how many steps ahead its features can still be predicted from the current frame and the actions. Then test whether encoders with longer horizons plan further.

Related works

  • Planning Limits measures the plannable range by training a world model per encoder. We want to predict that range without training one.
  • ALPS notes that single-frame readout quality and predictability under learned dynamics can disagree, but doesn't measure predictability over horizons.
  • DINO-WM and LeWorldModel plan in an embedding space but take its predictability as given.
  • Our stacking-encoders project already measures how well future features can be predicted from the current frame, at 4, 16 and 32 steps, on cached LIBERO features. This proposal reuses that code.

Method

  1. Embed trajectories with each frozen encoder (Meta-World for Planning Limits' 5 encoders; LIBERO for the encoders we already have).
  2. For k = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 and 40 steps, fit a ridge model from the current embedding and the actions in between to the embedding k steps later. Record held-out R².
  3. The horizon is the k at which R² falls below a fixed fraction of its one-step value. Report the whole curve, not only the cutoff.
  4. Rank encoders by horizon and compare with Planning Limits' plannable ranges.

Experiments

  • E1: horizon curves for D, L, R, S and G on our cached LIBERO features. CPU only, no new extraction.
  • E2: horizon curves for DINOv2, V-JEPA 2, V-JEPA 2.1, VideoMAEv2 and VideoPrism on Meta-World, compared with Planning Limits' Table 2.

Expected results

  • Metric: rank agreement (Spearman) between horizon and plannable range; R² curves with bootstrap CIs over trajectories.
  • Hope: DINOv2 and V-JEPA 2.1 have the longest horizons, matching their longer plannable range.
  • Against: if all horizons are similar, the encoder's effect on range comes from something other than linear predictability, such as the shape of the goal-distance cost.
  • Limit: with 5 encoders, this is a tally, not a statistical test.

Open questions

  • Is this a separate project, or a section inside proposal 2? It shares data and code with it.
  • Does Planning Limits release its data or rollouts, so the comparison uses the same tasks?
